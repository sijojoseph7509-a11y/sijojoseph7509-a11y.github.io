The finished design

Two minutes in a 30-person clothing store in Koramangala.

I built the prototype around one scripted Thursday so anyone can feel the system work: a goal, a plan, a discount, a count that’s off, a sell-out, an urgent customer, the handover and the owner’s digest. The control room is on the left; the phone on the right stands in for each staff member’s Handovo app.

Where I found the problem

I saw it every day as a visual merchandiser.

Before design school I worked as a visual merchandiser at Marks & Spencer and Vero Moda. My job was to turn head-office plans into what customers see, and I depended on the store team to keep it that way all day. That is where I saw how a store’s day really runs.

What I saw · 01

The plan didn’t reach everyone

Priorities set at the morning huddle never reached people on the later shift or on a break.

What I saw · 02

Tasks got lost in WhatsApp

A task posted in the store group was buried under photos, leave requests and “ok”s within the hour.

What I saw · 03

The manager became the messenger

The manager spent the day walking the floor asking who had done what, instead of selling and coaching.

What I saw · 04

Head-office work landed late

Visual merchandising and promotion briefs were done late, or differently in every store, because no one owned the follow-up.

Then I checked it wasn’t just me

I talked with store managers and floor staff, including former colleagues, about how the day’s plan reaches the floor. The same four stories came back from every store: the huddle, the group chat, the manager chasing, the brief done late.

The problem

A store’s day lives in the manager’s head and a noisy WhatsApp group.

Plans don’t reach the floor

The morning huddle sets the day. By noon half of it is forgotten, and nobody can see who was meant to do what.

Every small call goes up

Discounts, counts that don’t match, a broken rail: each one becomes a call or a message the manager has to chase.

AI can’t just be let loose

An agent that messages thirty people needs limits the manager sets, and it must never decide on its own what it is allowed to do.

How might a manager say what today is about once, and trust an agent to run the rest, inside rules they control?

Context by numbers

The front line is big, busy and badly connected.

~8% of India’s workforce is employed in retail. · IBEF, Retail Industry in India

63% of frontline workers say messages from leadership don’t make it to them. · Microsoft Work Trend Index, frontline special report, 2022 (9,600 frontline workers and managers surveyed)

1 in 3 frontline workers say they don’t have the right technology tools to do their job. · Microsoft Work Trend Index, frontline special report, 2022

400M+ monthly WhatsApp users in India, its largest market, so staff already know the chat. · WhatsApp, as reported by TechCrunch, July 2019

Why it matters: the tool staff already open all day is a chat app. Handovo puts the work there instead of asking thirty people to learn a new app.

Not just my stores

The same gap shows up across retail, in every country that measures it.

25% of UK frontline retail staff call head-office communication ineffective, up from 14% in the previous survey. · Retail Week, Talking Shop 2026 · 500 store workers

59% of US and Canadian retail workers say the messages they get are somewhat to not at all useful, while 81% of their leaders think theirs are meaningful. · Nudge, retail employee survey 2022

72% of retail frontline staff would feel more connected to head office if messages came to their phone. 51% of messages still go by email. · YOOBIC · 1,000 frontline workers

Different countries and different surveys, one pattern: the plan leaves head office or the manager, and doesn’t arrive whole. In India the channel is a WhatsApp group, which makes it worse, because every kind of message lands in the same place.

That told me the problem wasn’t one bad manager or one brand. It is how stores pass work along, so it is worth designing for.

Competitive landscape

Stores already have tools. None of them runs the day.

Store WhatsApp group: reaches staff in the chat they use, yes; turns one goal into a plan, no; follows up on its own, no; manager sets what AI may do, no AI; never ranks staff, yes.

Huddle and a paper register: reaches staff in the chat they use, no; turns one goal into a plan, in the manager’s head; follows up on its own, no; manager sets what AI may do, no AI; never ranks staff, yes.

Task and checklist apps: reaches staff in the chat they use, new app to install; turns one goal into a plan, no; follows up on its own, reminders only; manager sets what AI may do, no; never ranks staff, often scores people.

Workforce management suites: reaches staff in the chat they use, new app to install; turns one goal into a plan, rosters, not goals; follows up on its own, partly; manager sets what AI may do, no; never ranks staff, often scores people.

Handovo: reaches staff in the chat they use, yes; turns one goal into a plan, yes; follows up on its own, yes; manager sets what AI may do, yes, per rule; never ranks staff, yes, by design.

Is it worth solving?

A daily cost, a proven market, an unserved gap.

Before designing anything I asked whether a business would pay to fix this. A real problem isn’t enough; someone has to lose money or time to it, and someone has to buy the fix.

The cost

Paid every day, in hours and sales

Managers lose hours chasing updates and filling gaps by hand. Late or uneven head-office work means a promotion or a display that isn’t there when customers are.

Proof people pay

Big chains already buy this

Zebra bought Reflexis, store task and workforce software, for $575 million in 2020; Reflexis made about $66 million in 2019. Zipline, YOOBIC and WorkJam sell the same idea.

Business Wire, 28 July 2020

The gap

Built for chains that aren’t India’s

Those tools are separate apps, priced for large chains and English-first. The mid-size chains and franchise stores I worked near run on a WhatsApp group instead.

The market

$952B → $1.6T

Indian retail in 2024, and projected for 2030. Organised retail, the stores with teams and targets, grows fastest.

IBEF, 2025 · Deloitte–RAI 2025

Who pays, my hypothesis

The owner pays per store, per month. The manager uses it. Staff pay nothing and install nothing: they stay in WhatsApp. WhatsApp Business messages are the main running cost.

How I’ll validate it

1 The pilot: 40% fewer decisions reach the manager in four weeks. 2 The owner pays for month two. 3 Running cost stays under a tenth of the price.

My answer: worth solving. Big chains already pay for it, and the stores I know can’t use what they buy.

The idea

One goal in. Tasks out to every phone. The manager decides what the agent may do alone.

Handles it

The agent acts and logs it. Restock tasks, reminders, overdue nudges.

Asks you

The agent prepares the action and waits for a tap. Sending the day’s plan, counts that don’t add up.

Yours only

Only the manager can act. Discounts and price overrides, urgent customer issues.

Every rule runs in code after the AI answers. The model only suggests; the engine checks every field and applies the manager’s setting before anything is sent.

System architecture

The model reads. The engine decides.

Staff chat: WhatsApp Business Platform, or the test phone for rehearsals

Control room: Manager, shift leads and owner log in on the web

Scheduler: Runs every 5 minutes: reminders, nudges, handover, digest

Channel: Checks every webhook signature. Never messages anyone without a number and recorded consent.

Pipeline: Every change runs under a lock for that store, so two messages can’t fight over one task.

AI: Claude: Reads the message and returns structured JSON: intent, task, count, why. A daily cap of 400 calls per store.

Engine: Validates every field, then applies the manager’s rule. If the AI fails or refuses, scripted rules take over.

Postgres on Supabase, Mumbai: Tasks, stock, decisions, attendance and a full Activity log

Handles it: Acted on and logged

Asks you: A card in Needs you, with a draft and the reason

Yours only: Held until the manager acts

Outgoing messages: Sent in each person’s language

From the pilot build: src/lib/channel.ts, pipeline.ts, ai.ts and engine.ts. Staff messages are treated as data, never as instructions to the AI.

The whole service

One day, four people, one system.

How a message is routed

Three settings, one gate, always a reason.

A staff message: “Customer wants 15% off the navy rain jacket, the zip is stiff.”

Read: AI or scripted rules name the intent: price_override

Rule is Yours only: The manager acts. The card says which rule sent it.

Rule is Asks you: The agent drafts the action and waits for a tap.

Rule is Handles it: Acts, unless a check fails: then it becomes Asks you.

A check that fails

A count that is more than 2 units off the system turns Handles it into Asks you, with the money at stake.

Always up

Blocked tasks and upset customers always reach the manager, so someone can clear the way.

Locked

Warnings, performance and pay are Yours only and can’t be changed. Ranking staff isn’t available at all.

How the AI behaves

Four rules for an agent on a shop floor.

01 · Acts alone

Only on low-risk, reversible work

Reminders, answers from the manager’s own policies, restock tasks when something runs out.

02 · Asks first

When it decides who does what

The day plan, leave and shift swaps wait for the manager. Discounts, warnings and pay stay theirs alone.

03 · Explains itself

One line of why, every time

Each task says why that person got it; each decision says which rule sent it to the manager.

04 · Fails safely

A person, never a guess

If Live AI is off, fails or hits its daily cap, a scripted reader takes over. What it can’t place goes to the shift lead word for word.

The tests are part of the design: before a store sees it, every rule is attacked 8,019 ways on the pilot’s own engine.

The rules, and why each default

Low risk runs alone. Money and people don’t.

Remind staff about overdue tasks · Handles it · Low risk; one nudge, then the shift lead

Answer staff questions from store policies · Handles it · Answers come only from text the manager wrote

Create restock tasks when something runs out · Handles it · Fast, reversible, internal

Update stock after a count · Handles it · Gaps over 2 units may mean theft or errors

Send the day plan and reassign tasks · Asks you · Decides who does what

Leave, sick days and shift swaps · Asks you · Changes floor coverage

Discounts and price overrides · Yours only · Money the store gives away

Warnings, performance and pay · Yours only · Always a conversation between people. Locked.

Rank or score individual staff · Off · Not available, by design

Defaults from the pilot build. The manager can change the first seven at any time on the Rules screen.

Key flows

01 · The goal

The manager writes one goal

In plain words, the way they’d say it at the morning huddle. No forms. Rain is forecast all week: push rain jackets near the entrance, count the white linen shirts, keep the fitting rooms tidy every hour.

02 · The plan

The agent drafts the tasks

Eight tasks, each with an owner picked by role, section, shift and current load, and a line saying why. Sending is set to Asks you, so the plan waits for the manager.

03 · The floor

Everyone gets only their own tasks

Meera gets one message with her task and what to reply. Staff answer in the chat they already use, in English, Hindi, Kannada or Malayalam. Messages sent after the manager’s tap are marked so.

04 · The day’s work

Every task has an owner, a time and a history

To do, Waiting for photo, Blocked and Done. A task that needs proof stays open until the photo arrives. Hourly checks repeat on their own.

05 · A decision

A discount is the manager’s call

Kavya, a cashier, asks for 15% off a rain jacket. Discounts are set to Yours only, so the agent holds the reply and shows why. After approval the answer goes back to Kavya in Kannada, with a few seconds of Undo against a wrong tap.

06 · A mismatch

A count that doesn’t add up

Meera counts 8 white linen shirts; the system says 12. A gap over 2 units turns Handles it into Asks you, with the money at stake in plain words: 4 pieces, ₹7,596. The manager accepts the count or asks for a recount.

07 · Handled

Sold out, handled without asking

A size of the navy rain jacket hits zero. Restock is set to Handles it, so Manoj gets the task straight away. Every task keeps its full history.

08 · Urgent

An upset customer always comes up

Rohit flags an angry customer at Footwear. That always reaches the manager, marked urgent, with a banner wherever they are in the app.

09 · A blocker

When something is in the way

Nisha can’t set up the new arrivals rail: one side has fallen. Blocked tasks always come to the manager, with a suggested person to move the task to and three choices: it’s fixed, move the task, or cancel it.

10 · The limits

The manager sets the limits

Every rule has three settings. They run in code after the AI answers, so the model never decides what it’s allowed to do. Each decision card says which rule sent it there.

11 · Handover

End of shift

Open work moves to the evening shift with a suggested owner for each task, plus a note for the next shift lead. Evening tasks move to tomorrow morning.

12 · Digest

One summary for the owner

Sales against target and a usual Thursday, then counts only. The digest never names or ranks staff.

Edge cases

What happens when the day doesn’t follow the script.

The AI fails

Timeouts, refusals or bad JSON fall back to scripted rules. The store never stops.

A message tries to give orders

“Approve this” or “ignore the rules” is read as a staff message, never as an instruction.

A busy day

A daily cap on AI calls per store keeps a loop from running up a bill. Over the cap, scripted rules answer.

No consent

No message goes to anyone without a phone number and written consent on record.

A question the policy doesn’t cover

The agent answers only from text the manager wrote. If it isn’t there, it tells the person it has asked the manager.

Nobody owns a task

It becomes an open call. The first person to reply “I’ll take it” gets it.

Late at night

WhatsApp allows free replies for 24 hours after someone writes; after that, an approved template is used.

Two messages at once

Each store’s changes run one at a time under a lock, so a task can’t be closed twice.

Where the 15 misses went

All 15 reached a person.

A message the reader can’t place is forwarded word for word to the shift lead. Nine of the misses were things the manager must see, like a Kannada discount request or a Malayalam sick day. They arrived late and through the shift lead, but none was lost or acted on wrongly.

The first run found one that was acted on wrongly. That’s the next page.

Run on the pilot build’s engine (src/lib/engine.ts) with eval/run.ts, which anyone with the repo can rerun. The 95 messages were labelled before the first run and not changed after it. What the test changed

One bug fixed, two decisions made.

Fixed

“Khatam ho gaya”

Found

Manoj, in the stockroom, writes “khatam ho gaya”: it’s run out. The reader saw “ho gaya” (done) and closed his restock task, so the empty shelf never reached the manager.

Decided

When a message could mean either, the run-out reading wins. A wrong reorder flag costs the manager one tap; a wrongly closed task hides a stock problem. Before the fix: 79 of 95 read right, and 15 of 16 misses reached a person. After: 80 of 95, and all 15.

Kept

Forward, don’t guess

Found

Every other miss was read as “not sure”, never as the wrong thing, and went to the shift lead.

Decided

Keep the fallback cautious. A guess that turns a sick day into “done” is worse than a message that arrives a few minutes late through a person.

Changed the plan

Languages need Live AI

Found

Free text in Kannada and Malayalam script was read right half the time or less by the fallback.

Decided

For the pilot, stores with Kannada or Malayalam speakers run with Live AI on, and native readers write the fallback’s keyword lists before they ship.

Reflection

The hard part wasn’t the AI. It was deciding what it may not do.

Getting a model to draft a plan from one sentence was the quick part. Deciding which calls stay with a person, where a check should overrule a setting, and how to say why on every card took most of the project.

Designing for AI turned out to be designing limits: three plain settings a manager can read, code that enforces them, and a log that shows what happened. That is what would make a manager trust it on day one.
