Backwater Line

One map, one naming system and seven sign types that let a first-time rider choose the right boat, from the right side, in either language, without asking anyone.

Discipline

Information design · Wayfinding

Deliverables

Duration

FILM · 41 S

From the street to the boat, by day and after dark.

THE PROBLEM

On the pontoon, two boats are tied up, and either could be yours.

Water transit in Kerala is growing fast: Kochi Water Metro carried 50 lakh passengers in its first 29 months (KMRL via PTI, 2025). Its riders still ask at the counter. A local guide warns that two jetties are “a twenty-minute walk apart and only one of them is going where you think.”

Quote: cats of kochi, Kochi Water Metro guide (checked Sept 2026).

THE SOLUTION

Seven signs. Each answers one question.

P1 Which way to the boats?
M1 Is this my jetty?
E1 Where does it go?
G1 Which side for my line?
N1 When is the next boat?
B1 Where do I stand?
L1 Where do I get off?

Self-initiated project on a fictional network. My role: network diagram, naming, sign family, pictograms, renders and film.

SIGN FAMILY

Seven sign types, one code each, drawn at one scale. Malayalam first, English at equal weight.

AFTER DARK

Signs that work without power, and better with it.

Lettering, line discs, yellow heads

Retroreflective sheeting, ASTM D4956 Type XI (microprismatic): returns light to its source, so a boat light or a torch makes the words glow.

M1 mast, N1 board, F1 fascia

Also lit: LED edge light in the mast channels, a dimmable warm (3000 K) LED board, downlights on the fascia. If power fails, the reflective layer still reads.

Sign ground

Matte black, non-reflective, so only the information lights up. No glare off a shiny panel.

To check before building

Sheeting grade against IRC:67 (India’s road-sign code) and a dusk test at one jetty with five riders.

The human friction

On the pontoon, two boats are tied up and either could be yours. The ferry is the easy part. The moment of doubt at the edge of the water, with a crowd behind you and a boat about to leave, is the problem.

Today that doubt comes from handwritten timetables, jetty names that change from sign to sign, and no map that shows how routes connect.
My question: what would it take for a first-time rider to plan a trip with one change, without asking anyone?

03 — Rider journey

Her first ferry…

A first-time visitor in Fort Kochi, no Malayalam, one change to make. Six moments, six questions, six signs.

01 Where is the jetty?

The street ended at the water. At the end of it, a tall black totem she could see from 20 metres away.

02 Is this my jetty?

The name in Malayalam and English at the same size, and the two lines it serves. The ticket pictogram beside it.

“This is it.”

03 Which boat?

On the pontoon, two boats. Same colour, same size, both about to leave.

“Which one is mine?”

04 Which side?

She looked up. One arrow, one letter, one place name: the boat on the right.

05 How long?

The board gave the next boat in minutes, not a timetable to decode. She sat down.

06 Where do I get off?

On board, a map of one line with the next stop marked. She stopped checking and watched the water.

Every sign answered one question,
at the moment she asked it.

Precedents

1933

Harry Beck's London Underground diagram straightened the lines to horizontals, verticals and 45° diagonals, and marked interchanges clearly.
About 850,000 pocket copies were printed in its first two months.

Lesson

It was first rejected for not showing true distances. Riders didn't care: they needed order, not geography.
I kept that lesson, and added back the water so islands still make sense.

Graveyard of ideas

What I threw away, and why.
Each idea broke one rule: never rely on colour alone, never make a rider learn a code, one name everywhere.

Rejected through critique against those rules, not through rider testing yet.

Rejected

Numbered lines (1, 2, 3)

Real Kochi boats already carry big numbers on the hull. Line 3 and boat 14 on the same pontoon is a collision waiting to happen. Letters don’t clash.

Rejected

Colour-only lines

Fast for most riders, but it fails anyone with red-green colour-vision deficiency, and colours wash out under orange evening lights.

Rejected

English plus icons

Clean, international, and wrong: most daily riders read Malayalam first. Icons can’t carry a jetty name.

Rejected

QR-code timetables

Needs a phone, data, a free hand and daylight on the screen. Fine as an extra, never as the sign.

Three tries at the platform blade

The sign that decides “which side?”, read from 8–12 m while walking.

Boat 14 at a Kochi Water Metro jetty: hull numbers are already part of the scene.

Rejected

Try 1 · Every stop

Complete, and unreadable at walking pace. Riders search for one word in a list of eight.

Rejected

Try 2 · Arrow and colour

Instant, but only if you already know your line’s colour. Says nothing about direction of travel.

Chosen

Try 3 · Arrow, letter, terminus, two scripts

The terminus is the direction. The letter survives colour-blindness and dusk. Malayalam carries equal weight.

Key decisions

A diagram, but with the water kept in. And Malayalam and English at equal size and weight: most daily riders read Malayalam first.

Distances aren't to scale, so the next-boat board shows journey times in minutes, the thing distance was standing in for.

Naming & bilingual typesetting

Two scripts, one hierarchy. Neither language is the translation of the other.

One name, everywhere

Each jetty has a single name and a three-letter code in the source. Signs, map, board and announcements all pull from it.

Equal size, matched by eye

Malayalam is set larger in points so its body matches the Latin cap height; same weight, same colour. To check in print.

Transliterate, don’t translate

“Valiya Kadavu” means “the big jetty”. Every name is a Malayalam word riders already use (kadavu = landing, kara = bank, thuruthu = islet), written in English the way it is said.

What stays Latin

Line letters A, B, C and Western numerals in times stay the same in both scripts, so a badge reads the same to everyone.

First drafts, before the coded family above: totem, blade and board. Too English-first, and the board relied on colour for the line.

Environment & accessibility

Glare off the water, monsoon rain, salt air, a pontoon that moves with the tide, and riders who can’t see the sign at all.

Environment

Glare

Matte, non-glare faces; no glossy laminate on any reading surface.

Rain and salt

Specified in marine-grade aluminium with UV-stable print; text never printed on glass.

Tide

Blades hang from the moving pontoon, not the fixed jetty, so they stay at eye level at any water height.

Night

Internally lit; yellow on black 11.5 : 1, blue #3D8FE8 on black 5.7 : 1.

Accessibility

Touch

Totem carries the jetty name in raised letters and Braille at a reachable height, as India’s 2021 Harmonised Guidelines ask for key signs.

Contrast

High tonal contrast on every sign, checked in greyscale, not only in colour.

Step-free

The accessible pictogram marks the step-free gangway on the map and on the totem.

Hearing

Every “Boarding now” on the board is matched by a spoken announcement in both languages.

Specifications for fabrication. None of this has been built or tested yet.

Edge cases

Water transit fails in ways a bus map never has to think about.

CaseHow the system handles it

High water closes a jetty

Board shows “Suspended” with the nearest open jetty; a printed closure plate slots into the totem.

Power cut at the jetty

Printed timetable beside every board; totem and blades need no power to be read.

A visitor using Google Maps names

The same names are published to online maps, so what a visitor searched for is what the totem says.

Festival crowds

Blades stay visible above head height; boarding direction is repeated on the pontoon floor.

Boat changes berth at the last minute

Board says “Now from the other side” with an arrow, plus an announcement.

A new line D

Palette and letters leave room: D gets a fourth colour that still passes the colour-blind check.

Signs fade in the sun

UV-stable print, and a yearly audit in the sign manual.

Reflection

Accuracy is not the same as clarity, and a sign is only as good as the data behind it. The design work was as much a naming system and a single source as it was a map.
