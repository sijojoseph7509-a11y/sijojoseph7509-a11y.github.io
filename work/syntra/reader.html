The Problem

Choosing a perfume is one of the few purchases we make almost entirely through a sense we can't explain. Shoppers arrive knowing a mood: something fresh for work, something warm for an evening, something that feels like them. But the store speaks a different language: names, notes and bottles that all look alike. After a few sprays, the nose begins to blur what the mind is trying to compare. What should feel personal becomes a guess, and the feeling people came in with quietly gets lost between the shelf and the counter.

How might we help shoppers find a perfume that feels like them, even when they can't put it into words?

When every scent smells the same

Your nose gets tired after 3–4 perfumes, scents start to blur together.

Stores offer coffee beans to "reset" it, but research shows they work no better than fresh air.

What we heard, again and again.

Four patterns from our store visits, shoppers and staff.

01

People know the mood, not the notes.

Shoppers walk in with a feeling: fresh for work, warm for an evening. The shelf answers in names and notes they can’t translate.

02

Too many choices, too few words.

24 options turn into 3% bought; 6 options turn into 30%. A wall of bottles gives plenty to look at and nothing to hold on to.

03

The nose gives up before the mind does.

After 3–4 sprays, scents blur together. Coffee beans don’t reset it. The problem isn’t the nose, it’s smelling too many.

04

Nobody is there to translate.

Staff are busy or untrained in scent, so shoppers guess from the name, the box, or the last strip they smelled.

How might we let people turn a feeling into a scent they can find, without smelling a single strip?

Why these choices.

Three decisions that shaped everything else.

01

Why physical controls?

Turning a knob feels like playing, not like filling in a form.

A screen full of filters feels like work. A knob has a start, an end and a feel in between, so people answer before they can name the answer. Strangers at the exhibit reached for it without being asked.

02

Why ZARA?

Affordable, wide, and focused.

ZARA’s range covers every price band, and one brand keeps the system honest: every setting points to a real bottle on a real shelf. Nineteen scents are enough to feel like a choice and few enough to match well.

03

Why VR and AR?

People remember a scent as a place.

VR drops you into the world the scent belongs to, so you remember a garden or a starry lake, not a name. The AR ticket lets that world leave the kiosk and come home with you.

This isn’t just a ticket.

Hold the button for two seconds and a 58 mm thermal printer hands you your scent: its name, notes and a drawing made for that scent alone. “Your scent lives in this drawing.”

Six tickets printed at our exhibit on 30 Sep 2026: Femme, Vibrant Leather, Rose Gourmand, Wonder Rose, Nuit and Man Gold. Each scent family has its own drawing.

Scan it, and it comes alive.

Scan the QR with a phone camera and point it at the drawing. The drawing lifts off the paper with the bottle, and the scent’s ingredients revolve around it in 3D, so the memory of the kiosk goes home in a pocket.

Image tracking with MindAR in the phone’s browser; nothing to install.

66

tickets printed on 30 Sep 2026, between 14:00 and 20:12, from the printer’s own log.

Two weeks, one table, one wall: the circuit, the screen, the worlds and a backdrop that glows.

01

The circuit

An ESP32 reads five inputs: a mood knob, three slide pots and a push button. Each was wired and tested on a breadboard before it went into the casing. The knob is calibrated to its printed dial, so each mood changes exactly halfway between two labels.

D34

Mood · A10K knob

D35

Longevity · slider 1

D32

Expression · slider 2

D33

Occasion · slider 3

D27

Button

3V3 · GND

Power rails (never VIN)

It sends “V,knob,s1,s2,s3” (0–4095) about every 30 ms over USB at 115200 baud.

02

The backdrop

It started as a foam model of branching channels for light. We cut the channels, set an LED strip into each one over an MDF base, and set the screen into the middle, so the kiosk pulls people across the room.

Who tried it

Visitors at our exhibit, many wearing a VR headset for the first time. They turned the knob, read the screen, stepped into a world and scanned their own ticket. The printer’s log holds 66 tickets from that day.

We sawWe changed
