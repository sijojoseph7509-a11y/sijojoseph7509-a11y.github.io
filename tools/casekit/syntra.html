Fair to every bottle, for one point of fit.

I ran the kiosk’s own matching code across all 162 settings to see what our rules cost.

The input

162 settings

Six moods on the knob, then three steps on each slider: longevity, expression and occasion.

The score

4 weights

Mood 0.36, expression 0.26, occasion 0.22, longevity 0.16. Mood leads because it is what people walk in knowing.

The catch

2 of 19

bottles would never be shown if every setting simply took its best score. One bottle would take 15 settings.

What I chose

Share it out

An optimal one-to-one assignment gives every bottle 8 or 9 settings, so the whole wall gets found.

What it cost

119 of 162

settings still show their single best match; 158 show one of their top three.

Measured on the kiosk code

The trade-off

0.01

average drop on a 0 to 1 match score. Fairness cost one point of fit.

Next: let people type a mood in their own words and have an AI model set the four controls, then test with 20 people whether the match feels like them.

Would it survive a store floor every day?

The exhibit was one day. A store is every day: stock, staff, and many faces in one headset. This is how I’d handle each; none of it is tested in a store yet.

Hygiene

One headset, many faces

A washable face cover and a wipe between visitors. VR stays optional, so nobody has to put it on.

Stock

Only what’s on the shelf

Connect to the store’s live stock, so a sold-out bottle is never the answer.

Staff

It opens the conversation

The kiosk replaces the guessing, not the person: staff hand over the bottle and a tester.

Upkeep

Parts a store can swap

An ESP32, five controls, a TV, a Meta Quest 3 and a 58 mm printer. Any one can fail and the match still shows.
